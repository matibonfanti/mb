undergraduate thesis — non-verbal cues in FOMC communication

Bocconi University · completed 2025

Central bank announcements usually carry little surprise in terms of policy, but we listen to them to update our internal outlooks on what future decisions are going to be. As humans listening to other humans, we pay attention not only to the content of the words — a "hawkish" or a "dovish" speech — but to how those words are delivered. As in any conversation, we subconsciously interpret vocal cues: tone, pacing, nervousness, stuttering, fidgeting and other emotional markers.

But we tend to be extremely biased when evaluating these things, easily swayed by charisma, reputation or appearance.

So: what if you could train a model to quantify the emotional expression of central bankers during announcements, and measure how those variables relate to markets? Can subtle vocal shifts signal underlying sentiment and move asset prices?

To investigate this I built a dataset of audio and video from FOMC press conferences and used open-source models — emotion2vec and R1-Omni — to extract per-clip emotion vectors, then tested them against high-frequency futures reactions around each meeting.

The preprocessing and inference pipeline ran on GPU instances, with clip segmentation, feature extraction and the regression code split into separate stages. The code is on GitHub: matibonfanti/fomc-thesis. I'm planning to write up the methodology and results properly here.
