Beyond Words: Market Reactions to Emotional and Non-Verbal Cues in FOMC Communications

September 2025 · undergraduate thesis, Bocconi University

home projects reading list

Central bank announcements usually carry little surprises in terms of policy, but we listen to them to update our internal outlooks on what we think future decisions are going to be. As humans listening to other humans, we not only pay attention to the content of their words (ie a “hawkish” or a “dovish” speech) but to *how* those words are delivered. Like in any conversation, we subconsciously interpret vocal cues—tone, pacing, nervousness, stuttering, fidgeting and other emotional markers.

But we tend to be extremely biased when evaluating these things, easily swayed by charisma, reputation or appearance.

What if you could train a model to quantify emotional expressions of central bankers in announcements and see how these variables affect markets? Can subtle vocal shifts signal underlying sentiment and influence asset prices?

What I built

Most of the research on central bank communication reads the transcript. It counts words, tracks how often "uncertainty" appears, and models the topics a statement covers. That work is good, but it can only ever capture what was said. I wanted the part that never makes it into the transcript: the delivery.

So the project became an engineering problem before it became an economics one. I needed a dataset that didn't exist — every sentence Jerome Powell has spoken in a press conference, labelled with the emotion he delivered it with, and lined up against what markets did in the seconds that followed.

I worked from sixteen FOMC press conferences between May 2019 and May 2025, pulled from the Federal Reserve's own YouTube channel, and stripped the audio from each one. The first real problem is that a press conference is not one person talking. It's the Chair, then a journalist asking a question, then the Chair again, plus shuffling, camera noise and crosstalk. Before measuring anything I had to work out who was speaking when, and keep only the Chair. That's a process called speaker diarization, and it produces a timeline of the whole conference marked up by speaker.

With the Chair's speech isolated, I cut each conference into clips of roughly ten to thirty-five seconds, splitting on natural pauses so a clip never chops a sentence in half. Every clip carries a precise start and end timestamp, converted into a single time zone, because the whole study depends on knowing exactly when each sentence was spoken.

Reading emotion from video and audio together

The interesting part is what reads the clips. Older emotion recognition models handle vision and audio separately — one model looks at the face, another listens to the voice, and the two are stitched together afterwards. That works badly for someone like a central banker, whose whole job is to give away as little as possible, and where the signal is in the combination: a slight pause alongside a tightened jaw means something that neither channel means alone.

I used R1-Omni, a model built on top of DeepSeek-R1 that is multimodal by design. It watches the video and listens to the audio jointly rather than in two passes. Two things made it the right choice. First, it isn't restricted to a fixed list of emotions — it describes what it sees in open language instead of forcing everything into five preset boxes. Second, and more useful for research, it explains itself: for every clip it produces a written rationale for why it reached its conclusion before giving the label. That means a judgement about Powell's demeanour isn't a number appearing out of a black box; you can read the reasoning and disagree with it.

Running this was not trivial. Each clip goes through the model as video and audio together, on GPU instances, with the intermediate stages held in cloud storage so a failed run doesn't mean starting over. The pipeline is built in numbered stages — download, diarize, segment, infer, align — and each one can be resumed independently.

Lining it up against markets

The last step joins the clips to market data: one-second tick data on S&P 500 futures and two-year Treasury futures, so that each clip could be matched to what those two instruments did immediately afterwards. Equities and the front end of the curve react to press conferences in different ways, and having both meant I could see whether anything showed up in one and not the other.

That produced the dataset the thesis actually runs on: several thousand clips, each with an emotion label, the model's reasoning, a timestamp, and the price path around it. From there the statistical work is fairly conventional — compare what happens after emotionally non-neutral clips against neutral ones, control for what prices were already doing and for the actual policy surprise, and repeat across a range of time horizons.

One finding is worth stating because it shapes everything else: Powell is overwhelmingly neutral. Across sixteen conferences the model classifies the vast majority of clips as flat affect, with only a scattering of anything else. That is presumably the point — a Fed Chair is trained to give nothing away — but it means the interesting moments are rare, and rare events are hard to measure precisely.
